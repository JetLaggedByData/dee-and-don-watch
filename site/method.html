How this works, and where it fails

Thirteen SEPA gauges on the Dee and Don, each judged only against its own ten-year record. This page is the honest version: the method, the one event it was tested against, and the places it measurably got things wrong.

A flood takes 13 h 45 m to travel 82 km down the Dee

Tributaries peak first

Small, steep side catchments respond to rain in hours. The main stem gathers that water and carries it down slowly, so every tributary gauge crested before the wave reached the lowest gauge on the main river.

Two ways of timing the same reach

Peak-to-peak rests on a single sample per gauge. Cross-correlating the whole normalised hydrograph uses the shape of the entire event. Where they disagree, that reach is genuinely uncertain rather than averaged into a tidy figure.

The same wave, as space and time

Every main-stem gauge at its true distance from Aberdeen, upstream at the top, over the three days around the peak. Each gauge is scaled to its own rise within this event — dark at its lowest here, bright at its peak — which is the same normalised hydrograph the cross-correlation above uses. Percentile is not used here: in the largest event of the record every gauge pegs at the 100th for days, leaving no headroom to show the one thing this chart is for. The wave is the diagonal, and it is the same 13 h 45 m as the table, drawn rather than tabulated.

The gaps between rows are gaps. Nothing is drawn between gauges — the 30 km between Mar Lodge and Polhollick is unmeasured, and filling it in would invent the very thing this chart claims to show. Tributaries are left out because they sit on different watercourses, so they have no place on a distance-downstream axis.

The method

Every gauge is its own yardstick

Level is measured against each gauge's own datum, so metres are not comparable between stations. A rise is flagged when it is faster than 99.5% of that gauge's own hourly rises and at least 0.05 m/hr. A level is flagged above the 99th percentile of its record.

Why not a z-score

The first version used a median/MAD z-score — the textbook choice — and fired on 10.91% of all readings. These sensors resolve to 1 mm and the rivers register no change at all in 13–50% of samples, so the MAD collapsed to one to four sensor ticks and "3 sigma" meant a 3 mm rise in an hour. It was measuring the instrument, not the river.

Bad data never becomes news

A stuck sensor coming unstuck, one bad reading, or a stretch SEPA marks Suspect all look like a violent rise. Anything carrying a data-quality flag has its rise and level flags suppressed, and is drawn in grey as "not trusted" rather than reported as a river event.

"Provisional" is not a formality

Recent readings carry SEPA's code 254 Unchecked until a manual pass that runs monthly. Values can be revised or withdrawn weeks after collection. Everything here is provisional.

This is routing, not forecasting. It uses water that is already in the river, which is why it works at all and why it stops working when it does. No rainfall forecast is involved, nothing here is published as a live prediction, and for actual flood warnings the answer remains SEPA and the Met Office.

Limitations

What this cannot tell you, including where it measurably failed.

  • Not a warning service, and no forecasting. It describes what gauges have already recorded. A quiet page is not an all-clear.
  • Lead time is a fragile number. It is set by the earliest marginal detection, so it can swing by days while the detector barely moves. One gauge has reported "at least 72 hours", then 10.8 hours, then "at least 72 hours" again on identical raw data, as the reference record grew from five years to eight to ten — because one rise sat exactly on the threshold and its rank shifted by 0.009 of a percentile.
  • Thresholds depend on which years you score against. Adding 2016–2018 moved every one of the 26 thresholds downward, by up to 6.4%, because those two years were quieter. That is the river behaving differently in different decades, not noise that more data removes. Ten years is a stated choice, not a maximum.
  • The strict rise flag missed the biggest peaks — no fire at Garthdee, Park or Polhollick, three of the highest gauges of the event.
  • Travel times are uncertain per reach. The ordering is unambiguous; the two independent timings agree on only three of seven reaches.
  • Flags are relative to each gauge and not comparable between them.
  • Distances are straight-line to Aberdeen, not along the channel, so km/h figures are apparent speeds, not wave celerity.
  • Spikes are invisible at the live edge. The detector uses a centred window, so the most recent 90 minutes are never spike-checked.
  • The floor excludes slow water. The 0.05 m/hr minimum that fixes the quantisation problem means a slow-responding body can never raise a rise flag — which is why Loch Kinord was removed rather than shown as permanently calm.
  • Scheduled runs are best-effort. GitHub warns scheduled workflows can be delayed and some jobs dropped, and that they are disabled after 60 days without repository activity.
  • Level only. No rainfall, no flow, no forecast input. Rainfall (SEPA parameter RE, the same API) would let this explain rises rather than only report them — that is the obvious next step.
